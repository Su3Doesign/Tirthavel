Foreword

Four screenshots and a circle

This started with four images saved on my phone. A pale stone ring covered in knots. A lake city that looked like it had been growing for a thousand years. Two shots of a cliff where temples climbed toward a giant wheel under an eclipse.

They had nothing to do with each other — except that I kept going back to all four. So I tried to make them one place.

This book is that attempt: the world, the reasons behind it, and every wrong turn on the way. I’ve left the mistakes in. They’re the most honest part.

Sumanth

p.s. yes, I rendered thirty-odd frames of a brown wall. it’s in chapter VIII.

From the Book of the Wardens

I

Before the tide had a name, the land was a garden of rivers. Then the sea woke, and the rivers drowned, and only the mountain stood.

II

The ones who lived climbed. Each generation built a wall above the last, and the city rose in rings of stone. They called it Tirthavel — the place where you cross over.

III

On the summit, Ilaa the astronomer carved a wheel to hold the sun’s road: thirty spokes for the moon’s thirty days, four bands of endless knots for the four ages, and at its heart a lotus of eight petals, pointing the eight winds.

IV

Beneath the lotus she cut a door. Over the door she carved two words: न प्रवेशः. No entry.

V

Those who walked through came out on the other side of the summit, a step from where they began. The Wardens say they came back. Their shadows did not.

VI

For the door does not lead somewhere. It leads somewhen. Once in three hundred years the Hollow Sun sits inside the Ring, and then — only then — the door is a door.

VII

No one alive has seen it. The ivy has. It has been climbing the Ring for three hundred years, as if it knows.

II

Why a Ring?

वलयvalaya · a ring

Everyone asks. For a long time my honest answer was “it looked cool”. This chapter is the better answer I found later.

(it still looks cool.)

II · 2

Six reasons

1

The only circle in a city of spikes

Tirthavel is towers, cones and spires — hundreds of vertical lines. A circle is the one shape the eye can’t file away with the rest. It wins every composition without trying.

2

Time is round

Every culture I looked at draws time as a circle. Konark’s stone wheels tell the hour by shadow. The chakra has twenty-four spokes. Celtic sun crosses, rose windows, the zero. A ring is a clock without numbers.

3

It frames the sky

Half monument, half window. Whatever is behind the Ring — dawn, clouds, the eclipse — becomes part of it. A solid gate would only ever be one picture.

4

It reads at 2 km and at 2 m

From the sea it’s a disc against the sky. Up close it’s a book of carvings. Not many shapes work at both distances.

5

A door inside a circle is a door inside time

This is where the story came from. Once the Ring was a clock, the door in it couldn’t just lead to the other side of a hill — it had to lead to another when. Everything in chapter I grew out of this one line.

Konark: twenty-four stone wheels on a chariot for the sun, pulled by seven horses. that’s when the rearing horse in the reference suddenly made sense.

6

The oldest story I know is full of wheels

A wheel of the year with twelve spokes. A battle formation shaped like a wheel that a boy could enter and never leave. A discus held up to hide the sun. A chariot wheel swallowed by the earth. I didn’t go looking for the Mahabharata in this project — it was just already there. It gets its own interlude →

Interlude

Echoes

प्रतिध्वनिpratidhvani · an echo

I thought I was inventing a myth. Somewhere around day three I realised I was remembering one.

(it’s always the Mahabharata.)

the name came from here.
kāla + valaya.

कालोऽस्मि लोकक्षयकृत् प्रवृद्धोलोकान् समाहर्तुम् इह प्रवृत्तः

kālo’smi loka-kṣaya-kṛt pravṛddho / lokān samāhartum iha pravṛttaḥ

“I am Time, grown mighty, the destroyer of worlds, come forth to gather the worlds in.”

Bhagavad Gītā 11.32 · Bhīṣma Parva

Krishna to Arjuna, on the first morning at Kurukshetra.
(yes — the same line Oppenheimer remembered.)

Interlude · 1

Eight petals, eight echoes

The Lock has eight petals. I ended up with exactly eight things the Mahabharata had already put in my head — and one more in the middle.

1संवत्सरचक्र

The wheel of the year

Ādi Parva · Utanka in the serpent world

Deep in the serpent world, the young sage Utanka sees two women weaving with black and white threads, a wheel of twelve spokes turned by six boys, and a man beside a magnificent horse. Later he is told what he saw: the threads are nights and days, the wheel is the year, and the six boys are its seasons.

→ a wheel that is a calendar, with a horse standing beside it. my cliff reference has a rearing horse on a plinth next to the Ring. after reading this I stopped calling that a coincidence.

2इला

Ilā, mother of the moon’s line

Ādi Parva · the lunar dynasty

Ila, born of Manu — in some tellings a woman who becomes a man and back again — bears Pururavas to Budha, the son of the Moon. The epic says Ila was both his mother and his father. From Pururavas runs the lunar line, and many generations down it, Kuru: the Kauravas and the Pandavas both.

→ so the astronomer is Ilaa. and her Ring counts the moon: thirty spokes, one for each tithi, each lunar day.

3चतुर्युग

The four ages

Vana Parva · Hanuman to Bhima

On a forest path Bhima finds an old monkey lying across his way, too weak — it says — to move its own tail. Bhima can’t move it either. It is Hanuman, his elder brother, and he tells him of the four ages: Krita, Treta, Dvapara and Kali, each shorter and darker than the last. The great war falls at the dusk of the third.

→ the Ring has four bands of endless knots, one per age. the innermost is Krita. the outermost is Kali — and that’s the one the ivy has reached.

4मयसभा

Maya’s hall

Sabhā Parva · the hall at Indraprastha

Maya, the asura architect whom Arjuna spared from the burning of the Khandava forest, repays the debt with a hall for the Pandavas. In it Duryodhana takes a crystal floor for water and lifts his robes; takes water for a floor and falls in; and walks face-first into a closed door he was certain stood open. Tradition also makes Maya the one the Sun taught astronomy.

→ Ilaa is my Maya: an astronomer who builds. and her door is exactly the kind Duryodhana would have walked into. it looks open. it isn’t.

5चक्रव्यूह

The wheel formation

Droṇa Parva · day thirteen

Drona arranges the Kaurava army as a turning wheel. Of the Pandavas only Arjuna knows how to break it, and Arjuna has been drawn away. Abhimanyu, his sixteen-year-old son, knows how to go in — but not how to come out. He goes in alone. Jayadratha holds the breach behind him, and the wheel closes.

→ a ring you can enter and never leave. Kaalavalaya is its mirror image: a ring you may not enter — and if you do, you come out. just not all of you. (two pages on.)

6जयद्रथवध

The sun that set early

Droṇa Parva · day fourteen

Grieving, Arjuna swears to kill Jayadratha before the next sunset or walk into fire. All day the Kauravas keep Jayadratha hidden. Near dusk Krishna covers the sun — with his discus, in the telling most of us grew up with — and the field goes dark. Jayadratha steps out to watch Arjuna burn. Then the sun comes back.

→ a disc in front of the sun, and a door in the day that opens exactly once. that’s the Hollow Sun. I didn’t invent it. I just remembered it.

7रथाङ्गपाणि

The wheel in his hand

Bhīṣma Parva · Krishna and Bhishma

Krishna has sworn not to take up arms. When Arjuna keeps holding back against the grandsire, Krishna leaps from the chariot and runs at Bhishma with a wheel in his hand — his discus in one telling, a chariot wheel in another. Bhishma doesn’t defend himself. He calls out a welcome: to be killed by Krishna would be an honour.

→ the most famous broken promise in the epic is a man running with a wheel. the Ring is a wheel that stands still and waits. the thorns around it are the moment just before.

8कर्ण

The wheel the earth took

Karṇa Parva · day seventeen

Karna was cursed long before the war: in his hour of greatest need the earth would seize his chariot wheel. On the seventeenth day it does. He climbs down to lift it out of the ground, and that is the end of him.

→ the Ring’s feet are sunk sixteen metres into the summit, as if the mountain has started to swallow it. I didn’t plan that. I noticed it on day three, thought of Karna, and kept it.

◯सुदर्शन

The Lock · Sudarshana

Krishna’s discus · su + darśana

Krishna’s discus is a wheel that is also a weapon, and its name doesn’t mean sharp, or fast, or deadly. Su-darśana means “good to behold”.

→ that’s the whole brief, really: beautiful first, dangerous second. the lotus at the centre of the Ring is my Sudarshana. everything turns around it, and nobody touches it.

These are the stories as I know them — from the epic and from the retellings I grew up around. Where the tellings disagree I’ve said so. Any mistakes are mine.

Interlude · 2

No exit · no entry

This is the page where “why can’t you go in?” finally got a real answer.

चक्रव्यूह  the wheel formation · in: easy. out: never.

कालवलय  the Ring of Time · in: forbidden. out: yes — but when?

Abhimanyu’s wheel is a trap in space: the walls close behind you. Ilaa’s wheel is a trap in time: there are no walls at all. You can walk straight through the door — nobody stops you, the Wardens just watch — and you’ll come out on the other side, a step from where you began.

You just won’t come out when you went in. That’s what the shadow is. Your shadow belongs to the hour you left, so it stays there.

the rule, finallyno entry isn’t a lock. it’s a warning. the Wardens don’t guard the door from you — they guard you from the door.

How big is 156 metres?

Bigger than the London Eye. Twice the Qutub Minar. Fifty Konark wheels, edge to edge.

the Ring’s centre is 62 m above the door’s foot, so 16 m of it is underground. it’s standing in the mountain, not on it.

I put a 1.8 m person by the door in every early render. it’s the only way my brain believed the number.

Turnaround

every pencil drawing in this book is traced off the real model. the lines can’t lie about the shape.

11 m thick. it had to feel like you couldn’t knock it over.

the spikes aren’t in one plane. they tilt in and out, like a crown that doesn’t quite fit.

the door has columns, the rose window has a frame. it’s the only part that looks “built”. the rest looks carved.

No Entry

न प्रवेशः

Why can’t you go through? In the story: because the door doesn’t lead somewhere, it leads somewhen. In the design: because a door you can’t use is the strongest invitation I know. If you can walk through it, it’s architecture. If you can’t, it’s a myth.

the door is lit from the other side. what’s over there? (I’m not telling.)

IV · 3

The city ate the gate

My first blockout had towers almost a hundred metres tall. It looked like a theme park, and from the shore you couldn’t see the Ring at all.

The fix became a rule: nothing in front of the Ring may rise above the line of sight from the bluff. In practice, 24 metres. The Ring grew from 120 to 156 metres at the same time.

the city is supporting cast. it took me two tries to accept that.

Stone, Moss & Leaf

शिलाśilā · stone

Halfway through, I looked at a real photograph of overgrown rock and hated everything I’d made. This is the chapter where I rebuilt every surface.

V · 1

I grew my own textures

I couldn’t download a single scanned texture, so every surface here is made from noise — eight materials, each with colour, relief, roughness and height.

the rule that fixed it: darken the cavities, sharpen the ridges, and never let the grain be the loudest thing on the page.

Afterword

What the Ring taught me

Scale is a feeling, not a number. The Ring was 156 metres on day one. It only felt 156 metres once there were tiny arches, tiny trees and one tiny door under it.

Rules beat decoration. Moss, ivy, the sightline, the height limit — every time I turned a taste into a rule, the world got more believable and I got faster.

Keep the mistakes. I remember every failure in this book. I barely remember the things that worked first time.

You already know more stories than you think. I set out to invent a myth and kept tripping over the Mahabharata. The best ideas in this book are the ones I didn’t have to invent — only notice.

The door stays shut. I’ve been asked what’s on the other side. Some things are better as questions.

Sumanth

Colophon

Kaalavalaya & Tirthavel — a personal world-building project by Sumanth, 2026.

Built in Blender (Python, Cycles) and Unreal Engine 5 (Lumen, Movie Render Queue). The world is fully procedural: Python scripts build it in Blender, and the same layout is lit and rendered in Unreal. The interactive Ring above is the same mesh, running in three.js.

Typeset in Cinzel, Cormorant Garamond, Kalam, Caveat, Tiro Devanagari Sanskrit and Mrs Saint Delafield. The four mood-board images that started this were found online; they aren’t reproduced here, only sketched.

The Sanskrit is from the Bhagavad Gita (11.32) and the Mahabharata as I know it, in the epic and in its retellings. Where tellings differ, I’ve said so.
